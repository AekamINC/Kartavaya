What the market actually asks maps for

A principal engineer’s survey of map integration in systems like ours — competitor feature boards, community threads, vendor licence terms, Indian addressing reality, and the money. Read it as input to docs/plans/PHASE-7-territory-and-address.md: it confirms that phase’s spine, corrects four of its assumptions, and answers the three questions left open for the owner.

2026-08-26 · ~40 sources: vendor docs, idea boards, community threads, licence terms, Indian government policy · numbered 92 because CLAUDE.md reserves against a proposal 91

The one-paragraph verdict. The most-requested map feature in systems like ours is not a map. Ranked by how often it is asked for across every idea board and community thread that could be read, it is “plot my records, and tell me who owns which area” — plot-the-list plus postcode-driven territory assignment — and the second half is the half people actually pay for. Every serious competitor ships the plot; territory routing is what gets sold separately (Badger charges for it four different ways; Salesforce at $75–150 per user per month). Phase 7 is aimed at the right thing, in the right order. But four of its premises need correcting, and three are licence conditions rather than opinions: Mappls requires a logo, not the text credit 7.5 plans to render; its terms forbid using its map “with or near a non-Mappls Map” and forbid caching to avoid fees; content submitted to Mappls carries a perpetual, sub-licensable licence back to them — which is what an autosuggest call on a client’s address is; and Google’s India pricing is now 70,000 free events per SKU per month, billable in INR, so two of the three reasons written down for rejecting Google are factually stale even though the standing no-Google-spend rule still settles it.

How to read the evidence. Three grades appear throughout. measured — a published number from a primary source: vendor documentation, government policy, licence text. reported — a figure from a credible secondary write-up, attributed where it came from. claimed — a vendor marketing number, useful for direction only and never for a business case. 3The five failures every mature implementation has already had

What the write-ups, support docs and incident threads have in common. Each of these is cheap to design against now and expensive to retrofit.

1The bad-address bucket, invented too late

Salesforce publishes a support article for it; Veeva, Infor and Maplytics each publish their own. A record whose address cannot be geocoded does not appear on the map, and if the product does not name that state, the map silently under-reports and the user believes the number. Salesforce’s own guidance is that clearing coordinates forces a re-geocode — which only helps if you knew the record was missing.

For us: Phase 7.3 already gets this right for boundaries, with unmatched, unavailable and invalid kept separate. The same discipline is owed one level up, on contacts: routed, no PIN captured, and PIN claimed by no territory are three different failures with three different fixes, and a single “0 routed” hides all of them.

2Metered cost discovered in production

The pattern is well documented and always the same shape: cheap to start, brutal to scale. One builder reported on r/SaaS that the Maps API was 92% of the product’s cost and the AI 8% — the opposite of the plan. Published post-March-2025 worked examples put 2 million geocoding requests a month at roughly $5,050. reported

For us: irrelevant at today’s volume and dangerous at tomorrow’s. The mitigation is not a debounce — a debounce is a UX affordance, not a budget. Count autosuggest calls per org per day from the first commit, and make Mappls’s console the reconciliation source rather than the discovery source, because their terms make their own usage statistics binding.

3Storing what the licence does not let you store

Google’s service terms allow latitude and longitude to be cached for at most 30 consecutive calendar days, allow place_id to be stored indefinitely, and permit nothing else from Geocoding or Places to be used with any map. Mappls takes a different line and forbids caching done to avoid fees. Either way, a coordinate column with no provenance is an unenforceable promise.

For us: if a coordinate is ever stored, store geo_source and geo_fetched_at beside it in the same migration. Those two columns are what make a retention rule, a vendor swap, or a licence answer possible later; without them the only honest answer to “where did this coordinate come from” is a shrug.

4One vendor, no seam

We already have the scar. The SDK URL in TerritoryMap.jsx has been dead since August 2025 because the vendor changed its loader shape, and the failure was misread as a credentials problem. Independent guidance on geocoding architecture says the same thing plainly: an abstraction layer reduces lock-in and improves reliability, and the common mistake is over-trusting a single vendor without monitoring accuracy in your own markets.

For us: one services/geo/ interface with a Mappls adapter behind it. Not a plugin framework — one module, one function signature, so the next dead URL is a file change rather than an archaeology exercise.

5Geocoding on read

The classic N+1: a list endpoint that geocodes each row it returns. It is fast in development with ten rows and it is a metered outage at 200. Our list endpoints already truncate at 200, which would mask it exactly long enough to reach production.

For us: geocode on write only, never in a list path, and never inside a request that a user is waiting on if it can be avoided.

4India is not a geocoding market. It is a PIN-code market

The single most important body of evidence in this survey, and the one that decides how much of 7.6 is worth building.

The numbers that set the ceiling. Testing a leading online map provider’s geocoding API against a large sample of Indian addresses, only about 40% resolved to a geocode with 500 m precision, and performance degrades further in tier 2 and tier 3 towns. Only about 30% of Indian addresses are written in a structured format at all. reported — Kabir Rustogi, writing from Delhivery’s data science work Landmarks are the everyday addressing unit and are hard to resolve mechanically: roughly 10 million points of interest exist in the top 200 cities alone.

Three consequences follow, and all three are already half-recognised in the plan.

  • The UK postcode flow does not transfer, and the plan is right to say so. The market’s own behaviour confirms it: HubSpot geocodes from City / State / Country / Postal code rather than the street line, and Indian e-commerce fills City and State from the PIN rather than the other way round. The PIN is an input that saves typing two fields; it is not an address lookup.
  • Multi-district PINs are a known national problem, not our data being odd. A commercial address-validation vendor counts 19,959 active postal codes against 712,068 localities and warns that India Post adds, merges and retires codes as boundaries are redrawn. Our own directory finds 1,229 of 18,839 PINs spanning several districts and 51 that do not resolve to a single state. Those are the same phenomenon.
  • The industry answer to Indian addressing is not better geocoding. It is a new identifier. Two arrived in the last two years, and they point the same way.

DIGIPIN — the honest answer to “why can’t we have the UK experience?”

India Post, with IIT Hyderabad and NRSC/ISRO, published DIGIPIN: a 10-character alphanumeric code for every roughly 4 m × 4 m cell in the country, unveiled 27 May 2025, with the final technical document released in March 2025 as the foundation layer of the national addressing DPI. It is open source under an India Post GitHub organisation, and its two APIs are pure functions — encode a latitude/longitude to a DIGIPIN, decode a DIGIPIN back to coordinates. In August 2025 the Department of Posts signed an MoU with MapmyIndia to carry it into their platforms.

Why this matters to us specifically. DIGIPIN is the precision the owner was asking for when he asked for the postcode flow — and because encoding is arithmetic rather than a lookup, it costs nothing and needs no vendor. What it cannot do today is be typed: there is no address-to-DIGIPIN directory, so it is generated from a location, not recalled from memory. Recommendation: do not build on it in Phase 7. Do reserve the decision — the moment we capture a coordinate anywhere (a mobile check-in, a map click), emitting a DIGIPIN alongside it is a few lines and no dependency, and it is the sanctioned national identifier our vendor has already signed up to.

Address Descriptors — the landmark, formalised

Google launched Address Descriptors from India in 2024 and has since expanded coverage nationally: an API that returns up to five ranked nearby landmarks with a direction, chosen by proximity, prominence and visibility, exposed through the Maps JavaScript API and a place-ID lookup. It exists because Indians navigate by landmark, and it concedes the point that a coordinate alone does not deliver a parcel.

For us: not a build. But it settles a design argument — if we ever add one free-text line to an Indian address block, it should be landmark, not “address line 3”. That is how the country writes addresses and how the largest map vendor decided to model them.

6Licence and law — four things Phase 7 does not yet account for

6.1  The Mappls attribution requirement is a logo, not a text credit

The published terms say the “Powered by Mappls [logo]” shall be clearly presented by the User for end User(s) to see, and in no instance the “Powered by Mappls [logo]” shall be removed or hidden, with removal exposing the licence to revocation and damages.

Phase 7.5 currently specifies: “Boundaries © Government of India (data.gov.in) — GODL-India · Basemap © Mappls”, rendered as our own DOM and guarded by a render test. The GODL half is right and the render test is a good idea. The Mappls half does not satisfy the condition as written — it is a text credit where a logo is required. Fix the acceptance criterion before writing the test, or the test will lock in the wrong thing.

6.2  No Mappls map “with or near” another map

“Customer will not use the Mappls Map APIs & SDKs with or near a non-Mappls Map in a Customer Application.”

Overlaying our GODL PIN polygons on the Mappls basemap is data on their map and is fine. What this forbids is any future screen that renders a MapLibre, OSM or Google map anywhere near the Mappls one — including a mobile screen that falls back to a different provider. It closes off “use Protomaps for the cheap view and Mappls for the good one” permanently. Worth writing into the plan’s decisions table so it is not rediscovered by a future session.

6.3  What we send to Mappls, we license to Mappls

By the published terms, submitting content to Mappls servers grants Mappls and its affiliates a perpetual, worldwide, royalty-free, non-exclusive, sub-licensable licence to reproduce, translate, publicly perform, sell, publicly display, distribute, cache, overlay, process and transfer that content.

An autosuggest call is a submission. Phase 7.6 would send a customer’s partially typed address — a client’s real premises — to a third party on every keystroke after the debounce. Under DPDP that is a disclosure to a processor requiring notice; under this clause it is also a broad content licence. That sits uncomfortably beside the standing platform-privacy line that Aekam must not see client emails.

Recommendation: keep 7.6, and constrain it. Send the query fragment only and never the stored record; do not send it for existing saved addresses; do not run autosuggest on the public inbound form; and add one line to the privacy notice naming Mappls as a processor for address lookup. Also: the terms forbid caching done to avoid fees, so do not design a results cache as a cost lever — if call volume becomes a problem, the answer is fewer calls, not a shadow copy.

6.4  Two laws worth one paragraph each

Geospatial Data Guidelines, 2021. India liberalised mapping but kept edges: the threshold is 1 m horizontal and 3 m vertical; finer data must be stored and processed in India; terrestrial mobile mapping, street-view survey and survey in Indian territorial waters are for Indian entities only; and where a foreign or foreign-controlled entity licenses finer-than-threshold data from an Indian entity, access must be through APIs that do not allow the data to pass through the licensee’s servers, with resale prohibited. Our design proxies Mappls server-side. If Aekam Inc is an Indian entity, this is a non-issue — but it is worth confirming once, in writing, because the answer changes 7.6’s architecture from “server-side proxy” to “must be client-side”, and that is not a late-stage change anyone wants.

DPDP. Consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, and cannot be buried in an employment contract. There is an employment-purposes ground that covers a good deal of workforce processing without consent. Commentary consistently rates a GPS geofence that captures location only at the punch as more DPDP-friendly than storing biometric templates centrally — which is an argument for the Pahchan design we already have, and against demand row 12 (continuous tracking), which is the version with real exposure.

8The three open questions, answered

1When one PIN falls in two territories, which wins?

What the market does. Two different answers exist and both are shipped. Salesforce resolves with an explicit priority value on the territory, with the guidance that you invent your own scheme and make sure it scales as territory types are added. Zoho takes the opposite line — a record can be assigned to several territories at once if it meets the criteria for each. Practitioner guidance sits in between: overlaps are legitimate, should require a stated business reason, and must be monitored with a report for overlapping assignments.

Recommendation: priority integer, lowest wins, ties broken by oldest territory — and never block the save. Zoho’s multi-territory model needs a join table and changes every read path; at 17 territories and zero overlaps that is a large bill for a problem we do not have. A single priority int default 100 on graha_territories costs one column, is the mechanism the largest CRM in the world uses, and leaves the door open to multi-assignment later. Two conditions attached: record the losing territory in routing_reason so the decision is explicable months later, and ship addition C above so overlaps are visible rather than silently resolved. Per the standing GSTIN/PAN/TAN rule, an overlap must never refuse a save — it is a routing decision, not a validation.

2Should routing set the rep, or only the territory?

What the market does. This is the most unanimous finding in the survey. The documented routing hierarchy is: match an existing customer or open opportunity first, then a named account owner, then territory, then segment, and only then round robin — because inbound leads from existing customers or target accounts should never be routed blindly by geography or round robin, and existing account ownership takes priority over a general rotation. Specific rules must run before general ones or the broad rotation captures records meant for someone else.

Recommendation: the plan’s own answer is correct — territory always, rep only when unassigned. The market evidence backs it without qualification, and the live denominator makes it urgent rather than theoretical: 42 of 54 Unicode contacts already have an owner, so a rep-setting router would reassign live work on its first run. Two refinements from the same guidance: extend “already owned” to cover the contact’s client having an owner — inheriting the company’s rep beats a fresh round robin — and write routing_reason either way, so a rep who receives a contact can see whether it came from a territory rule or a rotation. Territory assignment is safe because it adds a fact; rep assignment is dangerous because it overwrites one.

3Should the public web form ask for a PIN?

What the market does. The field-count evidence is unusually specific. Reported conversion falls from 23.1% at three fields to 17.0% at five, then collapses to 11.4% at seven and 6.9% at ten or more — the cliff is between five and seven, where each added field costs roughly 2.8 points against 1.5 before it. reported Our public form has exactly five fields today (name, email, phone, company, message), so a sixth lands precisely on the expensive step. Against that, autofill is the strongest positive signal in the whole survey: Chrome measured autofill users abandoning at least 75% less often than typists.

Recommendation: yes — but as a six-digit optional field, not a sixth text field, and not before 7.0 has landed. Three specifics. Shape it as the cheapest field on the form: inputmode="numeric", maxlength="6", autocomplete="postal-code", optional, placed on the same row as Company rather than on its own line — six digits on a numeric keypad is not the same cost as a sixth prose field, and the reported curve is about fields, not characters. Pay the user back immediately: echo the district and state under the input once six digits are entered, from our own 7.2 directory with no vendor call — this is the pattern Indian checkouts already train people to expect, and it converts a cost into a small moment of competence. Never validate it into a blocker — same rule as GSTIN, PAN and TAN.

And the sequencing point that matters more than the field. A PIN on the inbound form routes nothing until territories carry PINs, and today zero of 17 do. Shipping the field before 7.0 collects a number that goes nowhere and produces the false impression that routing was tried. Ship it after 7.0, and measure it — it is the only claim in this entire document that we can settle with our own data rather than someone else’s benchmark. One last observation for whoever owns the form: if conversion is the real worry, the expensive field is message, not the PIN.

9What this research could not establish

Stated plainly, because a research document that reports only what it found is not a research document.

  • Vote counts on the HubSpot ideas. The community pages return 403 to automated fetching. The ideas exist and their titles and framing are visible through search; their kudos totals are not, so “most upvoted” is not a claim made anywhere above — the ranking rests on how many independent requests exist, not on how many votes any one carries.
  • Mappls pricing. Not published anywhere. The single figure quoted in §5 is a third-party marketplace estimate and should not be planned against. What our Railway credentials actually entitle us to is a question for the console, and it should be answered before 7.6 is written rather than after.
  • Ola Maps’ free tier. Two credible write-ups give materially different numbers. Not a candidate for us regardless; noted so nobody quotes either figure as settled.
  • Adoption data for map views. No vendor publishes what fraction of users open the map. The general SaaS numbers in §2 are the closest available and are a caution, not a forecast.
  • Whether Aekam Inc is an Indian entity for the purposes of the Geospatial Guidelines. An owner fact, not a searchable one, and it changes 7.6’s architecture. Worth answering once.
