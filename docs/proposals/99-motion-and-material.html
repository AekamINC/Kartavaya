Part I Motion — what moves A rigorous token system with almost no call sites.
01

What you already have is better than most of the market

This has to come first, because everything recommended later is small.

Five duration rungs, twelve named easing curves, and a build gate — check-motion.mjs — that fails on a raw cubic-bezier(), a bare time literal, a keyword easing, and all three again inside JSX style={{}} objects.

The real insight is the two-scalar split. --ix scales duration and bottoms out at .001; --motion-scale scales distance and bottoms out at 0. That is why infinite animations here do not strobe — scaling a 700ms spinner by .001 gives a ~1400 Hz flicker delivered to the person who asked for less motion. The reference spec mandates exactly that bug in its own worked example; this build refuses it.

Set Animations to None in the switchboard and watch the demos below go still rather than fast. That is the architecture working.

02

Why the bars do not move when you open Analytics

Two defects, one root cause, and the CSS looks correct in both cases — which is why they survived review.

Defect A. Bars, Funnel and Meters in pages/dristi/_shared.jsx are the primitives behind every analytics tab. Their fills — .dbars__b, .dfun__f, .dmet__f — carry a height or width and nothing else. No transition, no animation.

Defect B. Everywhere else — the KPI tile, the ageing meters, the workload bars — the CSS reads correctly and still never runs:

/* editorial.css:1734 */
.k-stat__bar-fill { transition: width var(--dur-xslow) var(--ease-fill) }

A transition interpolates from an old value. On mount there is none: React renders style={{'--w':'73%'}} and the browser paints 73% as the first computed style. Nothing changed, so nothing transitions. 24 fills across nine stylesheets animate only on filter change — never on arrival.

/* module.css — the analytics bar, corrected */
.dbars__b {

  transform-origin: bottom;

@keyframes dmGrowY { from { transform: scaleY(0) } }

scaleY not height, because the compositor animates a transform without a layout pass — the invariant animations.css already states for every keyframe in its registry. backwards, so a delayed bar holds at zero instead of flashing full. min(var(--i), 11), so a 40-bar chart does not take 1.5 seconds to draw.

No library for this. The charts are hand-rolled divs on purpose — _shared.jsx says why: they inherit theme, density and type scale, and a canvas would inherit none of it. Four CSS lines against ~31 KB gzipped.

03

The stagger that was specified, built, and never called

One orphaned keyframe, and a structural reason no gate can see it.

animations.css carries a reconciled motion table whose last row reads | list stagger | ixFadeUp | --dur-base, 38ms step, capped at 11 steps |. The keyframe is defined at line 178. Across the entire frontend — CSS, JSX and JS — it has zero call sites.

check-orphan-selectors.mjs explicitly skips inside @keyframes blocks, because a keyframe's from/to are not selectors. So an orphaned rule is caught and an orphaned animation is not. The build is green and the animation has never run. The other five ix-* utilities all landed; this is the one that would have covered every list in the product.

The fix is a ~30-line check-orphan-keyframes.mjs, both directions: a keyframe with no consumer, and an animation: naming a keyframe that does not exist — the second fails silently and takes a working surface with it.

05

The ground: seven surfaces, and one collapsed step

Relative luminance and WCAG ratios computed from the hex literals in kartavaya-design.css §7. Flip the Ramp switch to see the proposal applied to this entire page.

The live surface ramp · every band below is the real token

The whole light ramp spans 1.410:1. The dark arm spans 1.600:1 — 40% more separation, which is why the product reads with depth in dark and flat in light.

But the sharper finding is one step, not the whole ramp. --bg (#F3EFE6, L=.865) to --s-low (#F5F1E7, L=.881) is 1.017:1 — the two are the same colour to any eye. That step is page-ground meeting the first raised surface: the most-used elevation change in the product, and it is invisible. Its neighbours are 1.05–1.09, so this is an outlier in an otherwise reasonable ramp, not a systemic failure.

The proposed ramp solves for even 1.075:1 steps in the product's own cream hue, anchored to the existing endpoints. Full range 1.543:1 — close to the dark arm's — with ink contrast between 10.9:1 and 16.8:1 on all seven, so nothing goes near the 4.5:1 floor.

/* kartavaya-design.css §7 — seven hex values */
--s-lowest:    #FFFEFB;   /* unchanged — white pins the top */
--surface:     #FAF7F0 #F8F6EF;
--s-low:       #F5F1E7 #F1EDE3;
--bg:          #F3EFE6 #EAE5D8;
--s-container: #EEE9DC #E4DECD;
--s-high:      #E7E1D1 #DDD6C2;
--s-highest:   #DFD8C5 #D7CFB8;

The ceiling is real, and it is arithmetic. --s-lowest is already effectively white, so there is no headroom upward — separation can only be bought by darkening the ground. Even taken as far as taste allows, the ramp reaches 1.543:1.

So the conclusion is not "abandon the cream". It is: stop expecting value to carry depth on a light ground, and fix the channel that actually can.

My verdict on the cream: keep it

  1. It is differentiated, and that is rare. Every B2B competitor renders on white or slate. A warm paper ground reads as considered, and for firms whose mental model is registers and ledgers it is thematically right. It is the most distinctive thing about the product's surface.
  2. It is kind to read on for eight hours, while keeping ink near 15:1.
  3. Changing it is not one decision, it is 55 stylesheets — every shadow alpha, border and color-mix was tuned against this ground.

But make three changes: widen the ramp above; raise the light-arm shadows (§06); and introduce one vivid zone — an accent-gradient KPI strip or a tinted sidebar. Not a theme change. It is the one thing every glass reference has that this product does not: a ground worth blurring. That is §08, and it turns out you already have the token for it.

06

Liquid glass looks weak because it is weaker

You said it is not visible enough and needs more depth. Correct, and the cause is one declaration.

A .k-card paints box-shadow: var(--shadow-1). The glass rule outranks it at (0,2,0) and replaces the whole property with var(--lg-shadow), var(--lg-inset), var(--lg-shine). Compare what goes in and what comes out:

/* the house token being overridden — warm, heavier */
--shadow-1:  0 1px 2px rgba(30, 28, 22, .07);

/* what glass replaces it with — pure black, lighter */
--lg-shadow: 0 1px 4px rgba(0,0,0, .04),
             0 6px 20px rgba(0,0,0, .035);
  • The drop shadow gets lighter — .04/.035 against .07. On a ground with 1.41:1 of value range, halving the one channel that carries depth is why the effect reads as nothing.
  • The shadow turns cold. Every house token is a warm near-black chosen for cream. liquid-glass.css uses pure rgba(0,0,0) at all 26 of its light-arm sites. Pure black over cream desaturates it toward grey — it reads as dirt, not shadow.

That makes it slower, not deeper — you are already at 22px + 8px = 30px against a 2026 guideline that 8px delivers most of the effect. Blur is the translucency channel; on an opaque cream card there is nothing behind it to reveal. Depth here is a shadow problem.

A second defect, in the same file

liquid-glass.css contains zero occurrences of --ix and zero of --motion-scale. Its motion runs on --lg-dur: .3s — a literal, on no rung of the ladder — consumed by six transitions. So the OS prefers-reduced-motion path is honoured by a media query, but the in-app Animations = Reduced/None preference is ignored entirely: it writes --ix-user, and nothing here reads it. check-motion.mjs cannot see this, correctly — a value inside a custom-property declaration is its documented exemption.

--lg-dur:  .3s var(--dur-slow);
--lg-lift: -2px calc(-2px * var(--motion-scale));

With those two, the prefers-reduced-motion block becomes redundant and can be deleted — the same "token flips, not a parallel selector list" argument proposal 89 already makes.

Three levers, before adding a single selector

1 · Raise the translucency ceiling

one coefficient

Why frost reads as barely-there:

  • --glass-mix defaults to .6
  • --glass-alpha: calc(1 - (.28 * var(--glass-mix))) = .832
  • The topbar is 83% opaque — only 17% shows through; at the slider's maximum, 28%

Raising .28 → .45 gives 27% at default, 45% at max — visible, still short of the 40% floor that broke iOS 26 for text.

2 · Blur the scrims

11 scrims · none currently blur

The most recognisable liquid-glass moment in any Apple OS: a sheet opens and the world behind it goes soft. You have eleven scrims — .modal__scrim, .dr__scrim, .sheet__scrim, .k-modal-scrim, .kv__scrim and six more — and not one blurs its backdrop. They only dim.

One rule, eleven surfaces, no background changes, no contrast risk — a scrim carries no text. Highest impression per line in this document.

3 · Tokenise the nine strays

9 hard-coded blur literals

Of 35 backdrop-filter declarations across 12 stylesheets, 25 are token-driven and 9 are pixel literals that ignore the Glass intensity slider entirely.

.k-cmdk sets blur(20px) itself; the layer computes 13.2 + 4 = 17.2px and overwrites it at higher specificity. Turning glass on makes the command palette less blurred.

Dashboards and analytics

Mostly no. .anx-card, .vgw and .vgw-kpi are already glassed, and two of the three are non-surfaces from 89's P8 — .vgw is flex plus overflow:hidden, which clips the hover lift the layer gives it. Those come off; they do not get more.

But ViewGrid is a drag-and-drop dashboard, and drag creates a genuine floating layer with no material at all:

  • .vgw-ghost — the drop target. Already translucent (color-mix(--primary 10%, transparent)), already floating, sitting directly over the grid it will land in. The most correct frost surface in the module, and currently the flattest.
  • The carried card. A tile lifted off the grid is the one moment a KPI genuinely belongs to the floating layer — gain the material on grab, lose it on drop.

Reports

  • .gr__side — yes. position: sticky; top: 24px with its own scroll, holding still while the report body moves under it. Textbook frost.

The guardrail

  • Count simultaneous layers, not radius. Each backdrop-filter element re-samples every frame anything beneath it moves. A frosted sticky header over a 90-row table is one layer; all 18 Tier C surfaces at once on a 1366×768 integrated-graphics laptop is not. Pick six.
  • Anything bearing text needs a barrier layer — a solid low-opacity fill beneath, so contrast is a property of the component rather than of whatever scrolled behind. Precisely what Apple added in iOS 27 after the AFB letter.
08

The vivid tier — yes, and you already own it

"If glass needs a vivid ground, make the elements vivid." Correct. This section is that idea, costed and bounded.

The instinct is right and it is already half-built. kartavaya-design.css:990 declares:

--k-grad: linear-gradient(135deg,
            var(--primary-hover), var(--primary) 55%, var(--primary-vivid));

A brand gradient, derived from the user's own accent, so it follows whatever colour they pick with no extra work. It exists in both themes. It is used in twelve places — and every one of them is a fill between 4 and 8 pixels tall: progress bars, meter fills, bar-chart columns, the load bar.

You have a vivid tier. It has simply never been allowed to be a surface. That is the whole change.

One constraint, and it has a number

Promoting it needs one fix first. Measured against white:

StopColourWhite textInk textVerdict as a surface
--primary-hover · 0%#026B646.38:12.66:1White passes
--primary · 55%#04837A4.63:13.66:1White passes, just
--primary-vivid · 100%#05B7AA2.51:16.76:1White fails AA

So --k-grad cannot carry white text past its midpoint — which is correct for a 6px bar with no text on it, and disqualifying for a surface. check-contrast.mjs already records the sibling case in its own header: "--primary is a FILL at 4.04:1. Used as body-text colour it fails AA by a margin too small to notice by eye."

The answer is not to abandon the gradient. It is to add a second one, same hue family, deepened until white clears AA on every stop:

/* keep --k-grad exactly as it is — the 12 fills are correct */
--k-grad-surface: linear-gradient(135deg, #01554F, #046B64 55%, #05837C);
/* worst white stop 4.62:1 — clears AA body text across the whole sweep */

The rule that decides how much vivid

Look again at every reference you sent. iOS Control Center: vivid wallpaper, glass controls on top. Windows 11 Start: vivid desktop, acrylic menu on top. The referral card: vivid card — but one card on a page.

In all three the vivid thing is the ground and the glass is a smaller thing sitting on it. Never the reverse, and never everything at once. That is not a style preference; it is the mechanism. Glass works by contrast between two layers. If every layer is vivid and translucent, there are no layers — which is precisely how iOS 26 earned "Liquid Glass Is Cracked".

Where it must not go, and why that is not timidity

  • Behind tabular data. Ninety rows of rupee figures at --row-h: 66px need a calm ground and a working --on-surface-3. A gradient behind them puts every cell at a different contrast ratio, which is unfixable rather than merely ugly.
  • Anywhere semantic colour has to survive. --ok, --warn and --danger carry meaning on this data. An amber warn chip on a vivid teal card stops reading as a warning and starts reading as decoration — and that is a correctness bug, not an aesthetic one.
  • More than once per screen. See the right-hand panel above.

So the answer to your question is yes — with a budget. Add one token, promote the tier you already have from a 6px bar to a surface, and spend it on one zone per screen. That is roughly the same amount of work as the first four items in §13, and it is the change that makes everything else in this document visible: glass gets a ground, depth gets contrast, and the cream stays exactly where it is good — under the data.

The camera phases

--dur-base · --ease-emph · directional

Clock.jsx is a five-phase state machine — ready → framing → review → sending → done — and every phase change is an instant DOM swap.

  • ready → framing: the viewfinder opens — scale from centre, not a fade
  • framing → review: a shutter beat on --dur-instant is the one place a hard cut is right
  • review → framing on retake: reverse the entrance, as obBack already does in the org wizard
  • sending: needs determinate feedback — compressCapture walks a quality ladder to a 600 KB budget and that takes real time

The blocked wait

the strongest motion case you have

After submitting a photo the employee can do nothing until an admin approves. The one state where a person is genuinely stuck and the interface owes them evidence the system is alive.

  • Not a spinner — a stated status with the pending item shown
  • Approval should arrive with ix-flash, not on the next refresh

Consent — deliberately quiet

--dur-fast · --ease-standard only

The exception to everything else here. A legal decision under DPDP, and motion that makes one option feel lighter than the other is a dark pattern whatever the intent.

  • Agree and decline get identical duration, curve and weight
  • No celebration on agree. Ever.

obIn / obBack — already correct

Tabs and icons — what "modern" actually means

The cheapest change that reads as a generational upgrade.

Your module tabs do animate — .mt__b.on::after runs ixTabInd, a scaleX grow from centre, correctly avoiding a width animation. But it grows in place: the old underline vanishes, a new one inflates at the new tab. The modern pattern is one indicator that travels. Same frame budget; the eye is led from where it was to where it is.

Grow-in-place vs. travel

Today — ixTabInd

The underline at the old tab disappears; a new one inflates at the new tab. Nothing connects the two.

Proposed — one indicator, travelling

The same indicator moves, so the relationship between the two tabs is expressed rather than implied.

Roughly fifteen lines: one shared <span>, a transform: translateX() and width read off the active button's offsetLeft/offsetWidth, on --dur-base/--ease-emph. It replaces ixTabInd rather than adding to it.

Icons

You are on lucide-react with a consistent 16px stroke set in navIcons.jsx — the right foundation. Drop-in animated wrappers exist (Motion Icons, lucide-animated, Lucide React Motion), but copy rather than depend: they pull in motion/react, which puts their durations and easings outside check-motion.mjs — the exact blindness the JSX half of that gate was written to close. Take the three or four that earn their place — a check that draws, a bell that tilts on a new notification, a refresh that spins while fetching — and author them against your own tokens. And keep it rare: an icon that animates on hover everywhere is a page that fidgets.

What I would not do: replace the cream, adopt a chart library, adopt an animation library, or add neumorphism. Your own slide already gives the verdict on the last one — beautiful in a mockup, brutal in a contrast audit. You ship check-contrast.mjs; neumorphism is what that ratchet exists to stop.
