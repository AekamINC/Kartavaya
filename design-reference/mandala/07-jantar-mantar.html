जंतर मंतर यंत्र — six instruments

Jai Singh's observatories are masonry that measures. Every one of these is a real instrument at Jaipur or Delhi, and each measures something different — time, altitude, azimuth, declination — so the six are a genuine family rather than six versions of a dial. They are the most secular Indian geometry there is: no iconography, no symbolism, just graduated stone built to read the sky. In all six the graduations hold still and only the measuring part moves, which is what an instrument does and what makes them legible as loaders.

The instruments

Every scale here is real: tick spacing, gnomon angle and arc extent follow the instrument, not the layout.

Which to use

Samrat is the one people recognise — the giant sundial is the photograph everyone has seen of Jaipur. Its shadow sweeps, which reads as time passing more plainly than anything else here.

Ram Yantra is the best behaved as an ambient loader: sectors light in sequence around a ring, so there is always something on and never anything racing.

Digamsa is the quietest — one hairline pointer on a fine degree scale. It is the one that can sit in a table cell without shouting.

Jai Prakash is the most beautiful and the most fragile: its coordinate net needs room, and below about 90px the crosswires collapse into mush.
